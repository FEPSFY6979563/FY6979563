Abstract

        
Aerodynamic performance is crucial in Formula 1, but the Aerodynamic Testing Regulations (ATR) limit how much teams can test their cars and parts. This article explores how artificial intelligence (AI) is being used in F1 to improve testing with wind tunnels and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations. It finds that AI can predict drag without a full simulation, make simulations more accurate, and cut the number of tests needed from 25 to 12. However, AI still depends on CFD data and engineers, so it cannot work on its own and supports engineers rather than replacing them.


        
        


        
        
Introduction

        
F1 teams spend so much effort and time working on the aerodynamic design of their car and testing it in wind tunnels which have limited use (The Race, 2023). What if there was a way to support this which would be significantly more effective and efficient? Rapidly developing artificial intelligence (AI) is what makes this possible. This article explores how AI is being used to automate parts of aerodynamic design in Formula 1. Firstly, we will talk about F1’s testing limits and why they are limited. Secondly, we will talk about how AI can make predictions faster and make more use out of testing. After this we will then go more in detail about how AI is used in the aerodynamic design and how they make simulations more accurate. Furthermore, we will look at a real F1 example and then conclude with the limits of AI.


        
        
Background: aero and testing limits

        
Downforce is an aerodynamic force that pushes the car toward the track, increasing grip and stability. Drag is an aerodynamic force that acts in the opposite direction to the way the car is moving, caused by air resistance. It slows the car down and limits its top speed, so engineers aim for as much downforce as possible with as little drag as possible. To test their designs, teams use wind tunnels and computational fluid dynamics (CFD), which simulate the airflow over the car on a computer instead of using a physical model. However, both methods take a lot of effort and are restricted by the rules. Formula 1’s Aerodynamic Testing Regulations (ATR) limit the number of wind tunnel runs and CFD simulations that each team can do. Lower-ranked teams in the championship are allowed more testing than the top teams, which makes the competition fairer for slower teams (The Race, 2023).


        
        
AI making predictions faster

        
Because testing is limited, AI can predict the results, meaning that engineers don’t have to rely as heavily on CFD or wind tunnels to make predictions themselves. CFD simulations are very demanding on computers and take a long time, so engineers can only test a limited number of designs. Liu et al. (2026) built an AI that predicts a car’s drag straight from its body shape, without running a full simulation. It performed better than other AI methods and is especially useful in the early stages of design. If AI checks lots of shapes first, F1 teams can save their limited tests for the final, most promising designs.


        
        
How AI makes simulations more accurate

        
AI doesn't just make predictions faster; it can also make them more accurate. CFD simulations can't calculate every single small swirl of air, so they use turbulence models. These models have settings chosen by engineers, which are often picked through trial and error, and this can reduce accuracy. Bounds, Desai and Uddin (2024) tested this on the Ahmed body, a simple standard car shape. The researchers tried many different combinations of settings, then utilised machine learning to find which ones affected drag the most. This resulted in the AI picking the best settings. As a result, the simulation's errors dropped to under 7% on one shape and under 0.5% on the other. Integrating AI into CFD simulations would help F1 teams with their limited runs, because they can get more out of each run, so they don't need to use as many wind tunnel runs or CFD simulations as before.


        
        
A real F1 example

        
So far, this article has looked at how AI can be used in CFD simulations, but how is it used in F1? F1 provided AWS with a front wing design, and AI then picked which wing designs to test next, learning from each result. AWS reports that it found roughly 2.5% more downforce in 12 rounds of testing, compared with the 25 simulations normally needed (Hermoso Moreno, 2022). With the help of AI, the number of test runs can be reduced, as we have seen. This is very good for F1 teams because they can use their remaining tests on more types of parts meaning they get more out of testing overall.

Limits of AI and conclusion

        
Although AI is useful in aerodynamic design, it has limitations. The AI developed by Liu et al. (2026) was only tested under fixed conditions, and its accuracy was checked against CFD rather than real-world tests, so AI still depends on CFD. Similarly, Bounds, Desai and Uddin (2024) tested their method on the Ahmed body, a much simpler shape than an F1 car. Overall, this article has shown that AI can definitely be used in F1 to reduce testing and make aerodynamic design more efficient. However, AI does not replace engineers; it is a tool to speed up their work. As long as F1 continues to limit testing, AI will become an even more important tool for teams.
